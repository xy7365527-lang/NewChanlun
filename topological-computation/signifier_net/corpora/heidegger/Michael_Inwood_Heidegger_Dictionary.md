Insights on Michael Inwood's Heidegger


Insights from Chapter 1


#1


Heidegger was a German philosopher who lived from 1889 to 1976.  He was a priest when he first began studying philosophy, but he was discharged from the priesthood within a month due to heart trouble.  He then entered Freiburg University and studied theology and scholastic philosophy.


#2


Heidegger’s academic career was interrupted by World War I.  In 1915 he was conscripted, but regarded as unsuitable for combat duties and assigned to the postal and meteorological services.  In 1917 he married a Protestant, Elfriede Petri, and announced his breach with the system of Catholicism.


#3


He moved to Marburg in 1923 and began an enduring relationship with Hannah Arendt.  He published his great work, Being and Time, in the Yearbook for Philosophy and Phenomenological Research, edited by Husserl, in 1927.


#4


Heidegger was a German philosopher who taught at Freiburg University.  He was deeply attached to the provincial life of southern Germany, and he disliked big cities.  He lectured on the German idealists Schelling and Hegel, the allegory of the cave in Plato's Republic, and the Presocratic philosophers Anaximander and Parmenides.


#5


Heidegger was sympathetic to Nazism in the 1930s.  He was elected rector of Freiburg University in 1933, and in 1935 he spoke on The Origin of the Work of Art in Rome.  He began lectures on Nietzsche in 1936, which continued into the early 1940s and were published in 1961.


#6


Heidegger’s interest in technology began in 1938, when he gave a Freiburg lecture titled The Establishment of the Modern World-Picture by Metaphysics.  He was drafted into the Volkssturm in 1944 to help dig anti-tank ditches along the Rhine River.  In 1946 he was forbidden to teach.


#7


Heidegger’s career as a writer and lecturer soon revived.  He published On Humanism in 1947, and gave four lectures to the Bremen Club in 1950, one of which was delivered at the Bavarian Academy of Fine Arts in 1950.


#8


Heidegger died in 1976.  His last years were spent preparing a complete edition of his works, which was published in 1975.  He had declared his wish that no thought he had expressed in a lecture would be lost.


Insights from Chapter 2


#1


Heidegger’s book, BT, is the most influential of his writings.  It crystallizes the results of his reading, lecturing, and thinking over the previous decade.  It is a difficult book, however, and its overall structure and language present problems to the reader.


Insights from Chapter 3


#1


The term Being is used in several contrasts.  It contrasts, first of all, with knowledge and with science.  The term Knowing is used in relation to a knower and an object or range of objects known.  We tend to speak as if electrons were known in the same way as historical events.


#2


The world does not naturally present itself to us carved up into different categories for the different sciences.  The scientist is concerned only with one region of beings, and ignores the background against which projection takes place.


#3


Heidegger argued that we should focus on entities rather than on our knowledge or on the sciences.  But the verb to be has different uses or senses: the existential, the predicative, and the is of identity.  Why should we regard this as of central importance to the sciences.


#4


Heidegger argued that there were different types of Being, and that they could not all be classified under the categories of Aristotle.  He introduced a third term, how-being, to describe the mode, manner, or type of an entity's Being.


#5


Heidegger argues that not all entities are vorhanden, meaning that they are present-at-hand objects of disinterested description.  Some entities, like humans, are not vorhanden and cannot be described in such a way.


Insights from Chapter 4


#1


Dasein has a preliminary understanding of Being, and it is not a subject that can be discussed in isolation from other subjects.  All human beings have some understanding of Being, otherwise they could not engage with beings.


#2


The Being of beings, of other entities as well as of Dasein itself, is not independent of Dasein: theories, questions, tools, and cities all depend on the fact that they are produced, asked, used, and interpreted by humans.


#3


Heidegger uses the word Dasein to refer to the human being and the type of Being that humans have.  Our existence is different from that of other entities, as we have no definite essence.


#4


Dasein’s Being is an issue because it depends on the fact that this Being is in each case mine.  It needs to be addressed with a personal pronoun, I or you.  The Being of entities that are merely present-at-hand and are not therefore appropriately addressed as I or you is indifferent to them.


#5


Dasein, unlike any other entity, exists or has existence.  It has the ability to choose how it wants to be, and this is what separates it from all other entities.


#6


Heidegger's term for authentic is eigentlich, which means real or proper.  To be authentic is to be true to your own self, to be your own person.  Inauthentic Dasein conforms to what the they does and thinks.


#7


Inauthenticity is not a unmitigated evil.  It is the normal condition of most people for most of the time.  And without it, we could not make decisions.  However, it is possible for us to escape from inauthenticity by making a decision not to do so.


#8


The body is not a definite, inescapable what that every human possesses.  It is not something added to Dasein, or to which Dasein is added.  Dasein essentially requires a body of a certain sort, and is not a soul or ego that might exist in a disembodied state.


#9


Heidegger's focus on human beings as questioners, choosers, and self-producers is only one aspect of our existence.  He ignores any purely inner psychological realm or ideal realm of logical and mathematical entities.  His talk of Dasein's Being involves a sturdy realism that demotes, if it does not abolish, such disciplines as logic, psychology, and epistemology.


Insights from Chapter 5


#1


Dasein, the being that is aware of its own condition, is in the world in a sense that other entities are not.  It is not a self-enclosed subject, aware only of its own mental states.  It is a being that needs a world to dwell in.


#2


The world we see is not just a table, but the table in this room.  We do not first see it as an extended object and only later as for something.  We see it as well or badly positioned, as, say, too far from the light for reading.


#3


Space and time are involved in both situations, but with a different role from that assigned to them by Husserl.  He is interested in geometrical shapes and the shapes of our temporal awareness of our experiences of the table.  For Heidegger, however, space and time play a different role.


#4


The world and things within the world are usually inconspicuous to everyday Dasein.  This raises a problem, however, since philosophers are not a distinct species from everyday Dasein.  How then can they rise above average everydayness to become aware of what everyday Dasein fails to notice.


#5


Dasein’s approach to the things around it is a practical one of circumspect concern rather than disinterested contemplation.  It knows not only the individual items in the workshop and how to use them, but also the world and how to navigate in it.


#6


Dasein is able to understand the world around it because the world is spatial because Dasein is spatial.  Dasein cannot simply read off its sense of direction from the world around it.  The world is spatial because Dasein is spatial.


#7


Heidegger explains that our experience of others is not based on empathy, but on our understanding of Being and our being in the world.  We are always aware of the presence of others, and their actions and attitudes towards us, even if we don’t know the details of their appearance.


#8


Heidegger believes that moods are extremely important.  They affect our perception of the world and our interactions with it.  They are not within our control, and we cannot remove the pall of depression by manipulating specific entities.


#9


I walked to a nearby town, and sat down on a bench in the street.  I was overwhelmed with the thought of how far my sin had taken me away from God.  I was so unworthy to be in the world because of my sin against the Savior.


#10


The world is not revealed through moods alone.  For this we need understanding.  While moods are a vital source of insight for the philosopher, they are not the exclusive preserve of the philosopher.  Unphilosophical, everyday Dasein is prone to them as well.


#11


Dasein understands the world, things in the world, and itself.  It understands the environment as a whole and its own place in it.  It does not simply understand its environment, but understands it as presenting possibilities to it.


#12


When I interpret something, I do not simply stick a meaning on it.  I do not first see it as present-at-hand, as a length of wood with a piece of iron attached to it, and then interpret this as a hammer.  I implicitly understand it as zuhanden, as equipment, from the start.


#13


All interpretation involves a fore-having, a fore-sight, and a fore-conception.  The interpreter has in advance the object of his interpretation.  Heidegger, for example, had a preliminary understanding of Dasein before he began to interpret it.


Insights from Chapter 6


#1


Talk is the basic form of language.  It is about something, and it may involve assertions, questions, orders, and so on.  It may not involve grammatically complete utterances.  Silences are a part of talk as well as spoken sounds.


#2


Truth is unconcealment or uncovering, and Dasein is the primary locus of truth.  An assertion is true if, and only if, it corresponds to a fact.  But none of these items fills the role assigned to them.  We do not hear pure sounds when we are hearing another person’s assertions, and we do not understand what is said.


#3


Words have multiple meanings that vary depending on the context in which they are used.  The life of actual language consists in its diversity.  To restrict the meaning of words to a single, predetermined sequence of signs would be the death of language and the petrifaction of Dasein.


#4


Truth is not found in isolated chunks of the world that are separate from other entities and from the speaker and the listener.  It is found in the world as it is disclosed through moods and understanding.


#5


Heidegger does not consider his opponents’ views to be false.  He believes that their light does not reach far enough, and that they cover things up by showing things in a false light as well as by casting light in the wrong direction.


#6


Dasein is primarily engaged with things in the world, and it views itself as a thing.  It overlooks the obvious, what is too close to be conspicuous, and its own Being-in-the-world.  It submits to the power of the they and does, says, feels, and thinks things simply because that is what they do, say, feel, or think.


#7


Dasein has always already fallen from itself into the world.  It is constantly absorbed in its daily tasks, and the world in which it works is a public, not a private, world.  This leads us to view ourselves as present-at-hand, as a thinking thing, and as a tool.


#8


The they may say that men are machines, but that is not necessarily imposed on us by the chatter of the they.  Men may believe that they are machines because that is the model that most conspicuously presents itself to them in their dealings with the world.


#9


Heidegger argues that the philosopher’s concept of time and the self is implicit in everyday Dasein’s pre-philosophical understanding of these matters.  But how could that be, if everyday Dasein is wholly deluded.  Heidegger needs some clue to guide him to a conceptual account of being, and if it is not to be just his own private understanding of it, it must be an understanding he shares with others.


#10


However, Heidegger’s understanding of Being can be impeccably correct at a pre-conceptual level.  If it were so, how can we explain the fact that philosophers when they attempt to conceptualize this understanding often get it wrong.


#11


Heidegger’s philosophy is not meant to be adopted or passed on to others.  We should instead treat Heidegger as we would treat Aristotle, Descartes, or Kant: we should interpret and disentangle his work, and use it as a basis for new thoughts of our own.


#12


Dasein’s basic state of care is what defines it.  Sorge, like care, has at least two senses: caring and taking care of things.  Sorge is distinct from specific attitudes such as willing, wishing, striving, or knowing.  To will, wish, or strive for anything requires care.


#13


Dasein is a complex entity that is ahead of itself, wondering what to do next.  It is up to something.  It is already in the world.  It is engaged in a task, hammering, for example, or simply daydreaming.


#14


The first division of BT is about whether the external world is real or not.  Heidegger rejects the question, saying that it is not the world's failure to prove its reality that is the scandal, but the fact that people constantly expect and attempt to prove its reality.


#15


The world is not the end of the story, however.  It might not have been humans who existed, and there might have been no beings at all, as there were no human beings.


#16


If there were no Dasein, there would be no Being.  Without Dasein, there would be no world.  But there would be beings or entities, beings without Being.  They would not have any specific mode of Being.  They would not be present-at-hand like rocks and trees.


#17


The distinction between what is in itself and what is only for us is a distinction drawn from our own understanding of Being, not from the Dasein-independent nature of things.  If there were no Dasein, there would be no such distinction.


Insights from Chapter 7


#1


Heidegger argues that the word being is traditionally associated with time, when in reality, it is more closely related to presence.  He notes that common language involves more spatial than temporal metaphors.


#2


Time is special for Dasein because it lives its life in time in a deeper sense than it lives in space.  Dasein is born in a particular place at a particular time, and its place of birth does not limit its position in time.


#3


Heidegger’s solution to the problem of time is that Dasein’s awareness is not limited to the present.  It extends into the future and back into the past.  Dasein is temporal.


#4


Dasein is always ahead of itself, and it has a final possibility, death.  Death is not simply something that happens at the end of one’s life.  It separates the sheep from the goats, the authentic from the inauthentic.


#5


Heidegger’s third reason for introducing death is that it paves the way for his account of time.  Time is finite and ends with my death.  The future closes my ability to be, which is the future itself.  But the past is not closed in this way.


#6


Heidegger’s analysis of death is that it is certain, inevitable, and non-relational.  It is not just empirically likely or even empirically certain that I shall die, but I come to know non-empirically that I shall die.


#7


Heidegger’s account of death is that it is the end of Dasein’s existence.  But this is not compatible with immortality.  If Dasein is essentially in the world, how could it survive once it is no longer in the world.


#8


Heidegger believes that Dasein knows non-empirically that it will die.  But what it requires to exist as care need not be knowledge.  If I believe that I have about £100,000 available, I may manage my funds prudently, even if in fact and unbeknown to myself I have £10 billion or even an infinity of wealth.


#9


Death is not like loving, playing chess, or dancing.  It is more like solitaire.  It is not unique in this regard, as in dreamless sleep we cannot be related to others as we may be in falling asleep.


#10


Heidegger believes that when we consider the prospect of our own death, we are forced to consider the important relationships we have with others.  We can no longer remain dispersed in the inauthenticity of the they.


#11


Conscience is a traditional sense of the word that commands or forbids actions on moral grounds.  In this sense, someone who is entirely in thrall to the they-self lacks a conscience.  Conscience does not tell me what specific choices to make or avoid, but it calls on me to make a choice, take action, and bear my own responsibility for it.


#12


Dasein is Guilty because it makes a choice, and every choice has unforeseen and unintended consequences.  But when Dasein makes its choice, choosing its way of Being not for the next two days but for its whole life, it has no ultimate reason for making this choice.


#13


Dasein becomes resolute, entschlossen, and this means it becomes resolved about its life.  It begins to see its entire life from birth to death in a new way, and it begins to see the world and worldly things in a new way.


#14


Heidegger’s view of fundamental choices is similar to his view of truth.  There are no objective answers to life’s basic problems, and no decision procedure for resolving them.  The best one can do is to be resolute and make a decision in view of one’s life as a whole.


#15


Heidegger believes that resolute Dasein is better than irresolute Dasein, because it reveals itself, its possibilities, and its wholeness.  However, he does not believe that resolute Dasein is better than irresolute Dasein, because both are ways of Dasein’s being.


#16


Only the resolute can give an account of irresolution or of resoluteness.  To be a philosopher, you must first withdrawal from the round of everydayness, then rise above the current philosophical situation and the tradition that lies behind it.


Insights from Chapter 8


#1


Time is not a container that Dasein is in, but rather its temporality.  Dasein is placed at the center of things: time is Dasein's.  Each of these four notions of time derives from the preceding one, and Heidegger does not begin with the simpler phenomenon and construct the more complex one from it.


#2


Dasein, the subject of Heidegger’s book Being and Time, goes back to its past in order to make decisions about its present.  It cannot make decisions about its present situation unless it knows how it got to its present situation.


#3


Time is essentially and primarily about doing something.  For resolute Dasein, time is essentially and primarily about the future, which is the primary ecstasis.  For irresolute Dasein, time is essentially and primarily about the past, which lives on in the present.


#4


Dasein’s ability to traverse its entire life, from its birth to its death, is what makes it a unified self.  It bursts asunder into the past, present, and future, and then pulls itself together again like an elastic piece.


#5


Everyday Dasein is mostly irresolute.  It has its job to do and concentrates on that, without bothering whether this is a fitting way to spend its life.  It is absorbed in the present rather than the future.


#6


Dasein allows free play to occur between it and the world, and other Dasein.  It does not first encounter other beings, and then superimpose worldhood and significance on them.  It cannot encounter them at all unless it transcends them to a world and then returns from it to particular entities.


#7


Dasein’s transcendence depends on ecstatic temporality, which is not the same as other entities’.  Dasein has a future, a past, and a present.  It has aims and purposes, and it is not egotistical.  It transcends its own present state.


Insights from Chapter 9


#1


Dasein is free, and it asks why.  Because it views entities as possibilities rather than sheer actualities, it asks for the grounds of things.  It asks this because in its resolute, anxious moments, it views beings as a whole as a possibility rather than a sheer actuality.


Insights from Chapter 10


#1


Heidegger was interested in history from 1916, when he delivered his first lecture on the topic.  He was influenced by the historians Wilhelm Dilthey and Oswald Spengler, who believed that all cultures are self-contained, and that none have universal validity.


#2


Heidegger argues that there are three solutions to the problem of relativism: Platonism, Spengler’s acceptance of historical change, and a compromise that acknowledges a minimum of absolute values but only in a relative form.  He believes that none of these solutions is suitable for philosophy.


#3


Heidegger refers to the process of Dasein’s historizing as happening.  Dasein does not happen as a sequence of momentary experiences borne by a persisting subject.  It happens by running ahead to its own death and returning to its birth, by resolutely choosing one of its possibilities in the present moment of vision, and by adhering to it in self-constancy.


#4


Dasein’s choice of a hero is not unlimited.  Most people choose one of the roles handed down from the immediate past, such as a shoemaker, a priest, or a sexton.  Heidegger chose his heroes from among philosophers.


#5


Inauthentic Dasein is not resolute, but it is still historical.  Its historicality differs from that of authentic Dasein in two ways.  First, since it does not run ahead to its death and return to its birth, it does not abide in self-constancy by a resolution formed in a moment of vision.  Second, inauthentic Dasein derives its view of history and the past from the objects of its daily concern rather than from authentic Dasein.


#6


Dasein is always historical, though it can be inauthentically historical.  Not all Dasein is historiological – interested in the explicit study of history – nor are all ages.  Authentic historicality does not require historiology.


#7


Dasein has historical Being when it chooses a role from among those it inherits from the past.  However, even unhistoriological Dasein is familiar with various figures and events from the past.  When we are making a significant and irrevocable choice, we often consider ourselves to be crossing the Rubicon.


#8


An authentically historical approach is not limited to what past Dasein did or did not do, but to what it could have chosen as well as what it did choose.  The historian is concerned with the possibilities available, as well as with the possibilities that past Dasein handed down to us.


#9


Dasein is futurally open, and this gives rise to monumental historiology.  Dasein is also thrown, and this allows it to reverently preserve the existence that has-been-there.  This is antiquarian historiology.


#10


Heidegger considers Dasein to be historical because it is Being-in-the-world.  But Dasein’s world dies along with Dasein, Heidegger implies.  This is puzzling.  Two contemporaries are in the same world, but they do not conveniently die at the same time.


#11


The three temporal adverbs are then, now, and on that former occasion.  We use these in planning, and they involve the Present, which, in making present, says pre-eminently Now! Now! Life is made easier by clocks.


#12


Time is still public and significant, but it is now-time, which is not spanned and is infinite.  It is completely homogeneous, and it has no gaps or pauses.  It is completely irreversible, and it moves irrevocably in one direction.


#13


Time is derivative.  It does not follow that it is unreal or illegitimate.  It is natural for us to represent time, and we must do so in order to understand the past.  In order for history to appear as history, world-time is required.


#14


Heidegger and Hegel both believe that the path to truth is through ontology, the study of the being of the knower and the known.  But Heidegger believes that Hegel is the last and greatest of the Cartesians, while Hegel believed that man is infinite and destined to view reality through the eye of God.


#15


After BT, Heidegger continued to develop his ideas.  He never wrote the same thing twice, and sometimes contradicted himself, but his work was still coherent.


Insights from Chapter 11


#1


Heidegger’s first exhibit was a painting of peasant shoes, which revealed the existence of a world that the shoes represented.  Humans need a language to understand their world, and this was provided by the Greeks with their tragedies and epics.


#2


The establishment of a world is the work of Being itself, and Dasein becomes subordinate to Being.  Poets are the vehicle of an impersonal force, art, which is responsible for the creation of a world.


#3


Heidegger believes that the modern world is best conceptualized in terms of subjects relating to objects by way of representations.  He believes that this is not just a way of making or doing things, but a way of revealing things that precedes the making.


#4


Heidegger’s view of language was that it was not superimposed, but the original essence of truth as There.  It was the house of being.  The world was a realm of interwoven significance, and words were used to create an open space of entities for us to deal with and talk about.


#5


Heidegger’s use of language changes as his general view of language changes.  Early on, it is ordinary philosophical language.  In BT, it is still somewhat scholastic, though with some peculiar features.  He exploits the etymology of the words he uses and their connexions with each other.


#6


Heidegger’s language is more dynamic than standard philosophical language.  He prefers verbs and vigorous adjectives to substance-words.  He replaces man with the verbal Dasein, and space and time with spatiality and temporality.


#7


Heidegger believed that the idea of a Christian philosophy was as absurd as that of a Christian mathematics.  He did not believe in God, but he did believe in the existence of transcendence.


#8


We are not just focused on the thing in front of us that captures our attention at the moment, but we are always aware of the larger context in which such things lie.  We transcend not only to this or that being, but to beings as a whole and even to Being itself.


#9


Heidegger hopes that Being will assign him a role in promoting this revival.  The effects of technology, as he describes them, are similar to the effects he earlier attributed to Angst and boredom.


Insights from Chapter 12


#1


Heidegger was a committed Nazi, but he did not have to be antisemitic.  His published writings do not contain any overt antisemitism.  His relations with Jewish pupils were cordial and often amorous.


#2


Heidegger projected his own projectionism onto others, and blamed the Jews for their own destruction.  He also said that Slavic people would not experience the nature of German space the same way that Jews did.


#3


Heidegger believed that a groundbreaking philosopher is even more removed from the everyday world of philosophy.  He spends time with ancients such as Aristotle, Plato, and Heraclitus, who are not widely read by German peasants.
